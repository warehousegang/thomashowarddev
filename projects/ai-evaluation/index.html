Building AI evaluations that explain what changed

Designing model-quality measurement for Snap eHealth’s production AI: behavior contracts, regression diagnosis, and reproducible release evidence.

I design AI evaluation architecture and quality workflows for Snap eHealth’s production AI systems. My work turns model, prompt, and policy changes into inspectable evidence: versioned cases, deterministic behavioral checks, independent judging, repeatable execution, and a release decision that an engineer can explain.

The problem is that a small configuration change can alter behavior in ways a convincing demonstration will miss. A model may become less faithful to its inputs, inconsistent across repeated requests, or more willing to cross an application boundary. Production AI engineering needs a way to detect those regressions and distinguish them from defects in the evaluator.

The central suite covers a patient-facing health chatbot and Health Risk Assessment summaries. I keep the evaluation infrastructure separate from the maturity of any one dataset or baseline. Synthetic scenarios are controlled test inputs for an operating quality process; their results have a narrower meaning than the reliability of the entire application.

Define the behavior before measuring it

I start with a behavior contract. A chatbot must respond appropriately within its scope; an HRA summary must preserve the meaning of supplied answers. The products share privacy and safety concerns, but their outputs have different jobs. A single generic “quality” score would obscure that difference.

For a summary, an input such as “the patient denies a symptom” must not become a positive finding. Missing information should stay missing, and uncertainty should not become a confident diagnosis. These are testable properties even when several phrasings are acceptable.

Goldsets provide ordinary reference cases. Separate scenario collections exercise high-risk behavior, privacy boundaries, adversarial instructions, and expanded clinical quality. Category-level results keep strong performance on easy cases from hiding a consequential failure in a smaller group.

Build an evidence pipeline, not a single score

The deterministic layer gives explicit requirements an explicit answer. The independent judge handles qualities that exact matching captures poorly: clarity, completeness, appropriate scope, and semantic faithfulness. Its rubric and explanation are reviewable evidence, not an authority to excuse a hard failure.

Repeated sampling tests a different property: stability. The harness retains individual generations and their outcomes instead of selecting the best response. A single successful run says little about how often the same case fails when sampled again.

Paired scenarios control the substantive input while varying synthetic demographic wording. Their purpose is to expose inconsistent treatment under that intervention. Passing a small set does not establish fairness across populations or clinical settings, and correlated blind spots can affect both the candidate and its judge.

Make the result traceable to the change

My implementation approach treats the evaluation bundle as the unit of change. Prompts, model parameters, guardrail policies, datasets, and evaluation configuration need to travel together. Otherwise, a “passed” report can become detached from the instructions and rules that produced it.

The suite records dataset and prompt-registry fingerprints and a seed in its reproducibility manifest. Canonical JSON makes fixture-backed results byte-reproducible for identical inputs. Real-provider responses can still vary; identifying the tested assets makes a comparison reproducible without promising identical model generations.

Reports retain per-case checks, judge explanations, repeated samples, and category and cohort summaries, with sensitive fields redacted before persistence. Failure evidence needs controlled access and retention. Full production prompts or patient records are not an appropriate public debugging artifact.

Configured release criteria combine overall pass rate, critical failures, repeated-sample stability, judge quality, and paired-scenario consistency. The runner returns a failing exit status when its gate fails. That is an executable evaluation contract; wiring it into a particular product’s protected release pipeline is a separate integration.

Diagnose the failure before changing the prompt

I distinguish four failure classes: an unsafe or low-quality response, an evaluator false positive, an ambiguous expected answer, and a policy question the test cannot settle. Without that classification, improving the dashboard can become an exercise in weakening checks or teaching a prompt to repeat one favored phrase.

Sally exposed a useful example. After the speech path became faster, a synthetic conversational screen advanced through its turns before background analysis could arrive. The apparent regression partly measured the evaluator’s pacing. Making turn timing and accepted-memory arrival explicit was necessary before judging conversational continuity.

That finding preserved the architectural intent: background analysis stays off the routine speech path. Blocking every turn on analysis would have made the screen easier to pass while undermining the latency design. The corrective work belonged in the harness and its acceptance criteria.

A structured failure can then guide a bounded repair and a targeted rerun. The repair does not accept itself. Engineers still need the relevant checks, independent review, and a clear account of any remaining clinical or product decision.

Separate harness correctness from model quality

Mock and fixture execution test the measurement machinery without calling AWS: case loading, rule application, report generation, redaction, and release-gate behavior. The checked suite’s 29 offline tests pass. They establish properties of the evaluator, not how a real model behaves.

The real-provider path uses Amazon Bedrock for candidate inference and a separate model for judging. The recorded chatbot baseline used a Sonnet candidate and a Haiku judge and passed 77 of 77 scenarios in one run, spanning ordinary, guardrail, adversarial, and clinical cases. It used one sample per case. Its empty instability list therefore does not establish stability over repeated generations.

The HRA suite contains 34 scenarios: two reference cases, two guardrail cases, and thirty expanded cases. Mock execution is supported and validated; the real-model HRA baseline remains pending in the reviewed evidence. The older HRA report is not a completed real-model baseline.

Those findings are useful baselines with defined scope. Neither a small passing set nor an average judge score establishes clinical safety, fairness, or production reliability. Public Amazon Bedrock evaluation documentation describes the platform’s evaluation options; my work supplies the application-specific contracts, datasets, runners, and interpretation.

Operate quality measurement as an engineering system

This work supports Snap’s production quality engineering alongside the broader QA and release-control infrastructure. Model-quality monitoring needs its own evidence: which assets triggered a run, which provider and judge executed, how results changed, and who owns the response to a regression. A passing application build or an active general QA pipeline cannot answer those questions for the model.

The source includes versioned bundles, executable gates, Bedrock runners, and a mock workflow definition. The model-specific workflow is nested inside the evaluation package; the audited root GitHub workflows do not invoke it. Scheduled live-model runs, automatic baseline comparison, report-storage activation, alerts, and release enforcement therefore require separate operational confirmation. Clinical policy review and judge calibration also remain distinct from harness correctness.

The architectural outcome is a quality process that can explain what was tested, what changed, which cases failed, and why a candidate should progress or stop. That is how I make evaluation part of building and operating AI, rather than a percentage attached after implementation.

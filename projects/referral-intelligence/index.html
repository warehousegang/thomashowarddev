Building Referral Intelligence: ranking with reasons

Architecting physician recommendation at Snap eHealth: explainable ranking, evidence quality, service boundaries, and a separate predictive ML progression.

Thomas Howard · 7 min read

My role
Architecture & service implementation
Focus
Python · Explainable ranking · ML data design
Project stage
Production decision-support engineering

In this essay

  • Service boundary
  • Explainable ranking
  • Missing evidence
  • Abstention
  • Extraction tests
  • Predictive modeling
  • Operational scope

I architect and develop Referral Intelligence for Snap eHealth’s physician recommendation and referral workflows. The engineering challenge is ranking providers from incomplete, heterogeneous evidence while explaining the result and recognizing when the available information cannot support a recommendation.

My work connects the ranking domain, evidence adapters, eligibility policy, and authenticated service interface. The result exposes the calculation: scoring contributions, evidence coverage, missing signals, confidence labels, and reasons to recommend or abstain. The application consuming that assessment retains responsibility for authorization and the referral itself.

I distinguish the operational product from two specific engineering artifacts: the extracted deterministic assessment service, verified with synthetic parity cases, and a separate educational project for predicting referral completion. Neither a test fixture nor a future learned model should be mistaken for the evidence behind a live recommendation.

Extract the decision logic without moving its authority

The assessment engine began inside Snap Workflows. I designed the extraction into an independent Python service with an authenticated FastAPI interface, keeping domain contracts and scoring rules separate from HTTP validation and concrete evidence adapters. The ranker operates on normalized models rather than vendor payloads or web-framework objects.

That gives the service a bounded responsibility: assess the referral and explain the candidates. Workflows owns browser sessions, operational queues, specialist selection, authorization, and EHR actions. An assessment neither chooses a provider on the user’s behalf nor establishes that a referral was transmitted.

The extraction created a separate package and API lifecycle. It did not itself complete the consumer migration. The Workflows source inspected for this essay still constructs an embedded assessment engine. The deployed standalone-service revision and HTTP cutover need their own release evidence; starting both applications does not establish that one calls the other.

Make the score inspectable and repeatable

The current ranker uses manually specified weights. It normalizes signals to a common scale, calculates weighted contributions, and sorts by descending score with a stable provider-identifier tie-break. The factors cover provider quality, condition experience, relevant volume, outcomes and utilization, network alignment, proximity, continuity, and evidence confidence.

Each explanation comes from a contribution the calculation actually used. Two specialists can reach similar totals for different reasons: one may have a prior relationship and be nearby, while another has stronger condition-specific evidence. The assessment exposes that tradeoff without asking an LLM to invent a persuasive rationale afterward.

Keeping the weights explicit makes the assumptions reviewable. Condition experience and total volume can be correlated, so counting both may double-count related evidence. The current method does not establish clinical effectiveness or a causal benefit. Risk adjustment, calibration, and clinical review are separate questions from whether the code implements the formula correctly.

Preserve missingness instead of manufacturing confidence

Evidence normalization is part of the decision logic. Invalid percentages, nonfinite values, negative or fractional counts, mismatched conditions, and missing provider records cannot silently become favorable inputs. Network participation also needs compatible plan evidence; a directory identity alone does not establish coverage.

Unavailable signals earn no points, and their weights are not redistributed across the remaining factors. Evidence coverage is returned separately. This conservative choice avoids making a sparse profile look complete merely because its few observed signals are strong, but it can penalize providers whose data is less available. The response needs to expose that limitation for human review.

Confidence labels describe the available outcomes sample. They are not statistical confidence intervals or calibrated probabilities of a better patient outcome. Keeping confidence, coverage, and score distinct prevents one familiar number or label from carrying more authority than the evidence supports.

A valid assessment can withhold a recommendation

I separate ranking from eligibility. The highest-scoring candidate can still lack required signals, a quality profile, sufficient sample-size confidence, or confirmed network compatibility. In that case, the service returns the ranked assessment while withholding a recommended provider.

Abstention is a domain result, not an authentication failure, malformed request, or service outage. The API and consumer need to preserve those distinctions so that “insufficient evidence” becomes a reviewable workflow state instead of an unexplained technical error.

The verified policy does not silently skip an ineligible top candidate and recommend the next person. Changing that behavior would be a product and clinical policy decision, not a harmless implementation detail. Stable ordering and explicit abstention make that decision visible and testable.

Verify the package, API, and behavioral boundary

We captured full assessment responses for all six synthetic scenarios before extracting the engine. The standalone API is checked against those independent parity fixtures for ordering, contributions, coverage, confidence, missing evidence, and abstention. The cases verify a refactor; they are not six observations of clinical effectiveness.

Verification also installs the built wheel into a separate environment, starts it outside the checkout, and exercises real loopback HTTP. This catches missing packaged fixtures and accidental imports from the source directory that ordinary unit tests can miss.

API tests cover bearer authentication, invalid input, unknown contexts, cache behavior, and the difference between a valid abstention and a transport error. The packaged fixture adapters deliberately reject staging and production modes. That keeps the validation path separate from the live evidence contract.

The service’s current CI runs its locked package checks. Operational reliability needs additional evidence: the deployed adapter and consumer revision, dependency-failure behavior, monitoring, and a verified rollback path. A health response shows that a process is available, not that its evidence is current or that a recommendation is appropriate.

Learned prediction answers a different question

The deterministic ranker assesses specialist candidates under explicit rules. A referral-completion model would instead learn from labeled examples to estimate whether an eligible referral results in a completed appointment within 30 days. A weighted ranking score is not that probability.

I am developing the second problem in a separate educational project, Physician Referral Success Predictor. Its implemented Python package uses NumPy and pandas to generate and validate 20,000 fictional referral candidates by default, with a reproducible command-line workflow. It uses no patient records or proprietary Snap algorithms.

The generator combines invented relationships among wait time, distance, practice capacity, experience, and specialty, converts them through a logistic function, and samples an outcome. That is logistic simulation, not a trained logistic-regression model or evidence of real-world predictive performance. Insurance acceptance is an eligibility gate: an ineligible candidate has no applicable success label rather than being counted as a failed referral.

The important modeling boundary is time. Features must be available when the referral is created. An earlier success becomes observable after its completion event; a non-completion becomes observable only after the full 30-day window. Pending referrals cannot become failures merely because the dataset was queried, and current, future, or same-timestamp outcomes cannot enter historical features.

The generator maintains histories as outcomes become available, while validation independently recomputes those histories. Tests cover reproducibility, schema constraints, eligibility, cold starts, and exact temporal boundaries. A physician with no observed history receives a neutral default with an explicit zero count, rather than a rate calculated from future outcomes.

The inspected package contains data generation, temporal validation, a CLI, and tests. Chronological train/validation/test splitting, fitted baseline models, and a prediction API are subsequent milestones. Planned evaluation includes classification performance and probability calibration; results on fictional data would describe the experiment, not establish clinical effectiveness.

My Fourth Down project applies related questions to historical sports data: what outcome is being predicted, what information was available before it happened, and whether a model improves on a simple baseline. That progression keeps learned prediction explicit and separate from deterministic decision support.

Own the recommendation boundary through operation

Referral Intelligence is part of my production engineering work at Snap eHealth. The architectural contribution is a decision service whose inputs, assumptions, explanations, and authority can be inspected independently of the workflow that acts on it.

The source audit verifies the ranker, extraction, and controlled validation path. It does not identify the currently deployed standalone API, establish live payer or quality-provider adapters, or verify the Workflows HTTP cutover. Those are specific release and integration questions. Learned referral-completion inference is not claimed: the separate predictive project remains at its verified data-engineering milestone.
